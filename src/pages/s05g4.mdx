		# Introduction — Against All Odds: Filipinos Overcoming Adversities

		Long before "Silicon Valley" entered the Filipino vocabulary, Filipino inventors and engineers were already solving problems with whatever resources they had. Many of the figures in this exhibit started far from any tech hub — a farming town in Cagayan, a small city in Batangas — and reached the frontier of computing and electronics through scholarships, sheer persistence, and a willingness to leave home. Their stories are not just about invention; they are about adversity, access, and the long road between talent and opportunity.

		## Chapter 1 — The Pioneers: First Filipinos in Tech

		Gregorio Y. Zara (1902–1978) is often cited as the country's earliest tech pioneer of global significance. A National Scientist trained at MIT, the University of Michigan, and the Sorbonne, Zara patented the first two-way videophone in 1955, decades before videoconferencing became commonplace. He held roughly 30 patents across aeronautics, solar energy, and physics. His work illustrates that Filipino contributions to computing-adjacent technology predate the personal computer era by decades.

		## Chapter 2 — The Institutions: Philippine Educational Institutions Who Shaped Pioneers

		Formal computing education in the Philippines traces back to the University of the Philippines Diliman, where the UP Computer Center was established in 1966 and began operations in 1968 with the university's first IBM 360/40 mainframe, funded partly by the Ford Foundation. This center became the seed of the country's first Computer Science curriculum. Other institutions followed, formalizing their own computing programs through the 1970s and 80s.

		## Chapter 3 — The Overseas: Filipinos Who Contributed Globally

		Diosdado "Dado" Banatao (1946–2025) rose from a rice-farming family in Iguig, Cagayan to become one of the most influential engineers in Silicon Valley's early personal-computer era. He is credited with the first 10-Mbit Ethernet CMOS chip, the first chipset compatible with IBM's PC/XT and PC/AT, and an early GUI accelerator chip through S3 Graphics. Alongside him, Sheila Lirio Marcelo built Care.com into a leading U.S. care-services platform, becoming one of the most prominent Filipina founders in American tech.

		## Chapter 4 — Coming Home: Filipino Talent Returning and Building Locally

		Not every pioneer stayed abroad. Winston Damarillo, after building companies later acquired by IBM and Iona Technologies, returned to found Morphlabs and later served as Chief Strategy Officer at PLDT, bringing cloud-computing expertise back into Philippine telecom infrastructure. Paul Rivera co-founded Kalibrr, a job-matching platform that became the first Philippine company accepted into Y Combinator, demonstrating that globally competitive startups could be built and based in Manila.

		## Chapter 5 — Modern Pioneers: Filipinos Who Have Contributed to Tech in the Modern Era

		Martha Sazon, President and CEO of Mynt (GCash), has led the platform's growth into a central pillar of financial inclusion in the Philippines, with tens of millions of registered users. Her leadership reflects a newer generation of Filipino tech pioneers focused on fintech and digital inclusion rather than hardware — a shift in what "pioneering" looks like in the modern era.
