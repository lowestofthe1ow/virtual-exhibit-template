# ARM Architecture

ARM (Advanced RISC Machine) CPU architecture is a family of Reduced Instruction Set Computing (RISC) architectures utilised to specify a set of rules that dictate how the hardware works when a particular instruction is executed. (Arm Ltd., n.d.)  Created by ARM Holdings, then founded as Advanced RISC Machines Ltd. in November 1990 as a joint venture between  Acorn Computers, Apple Computer (now Apple Inc.), and VLSI Technology (now NXP Semiconductors N.V); ARM underpins almost every electronic system with its efficient architecture, having shipped more than 350 billion chips across the technological market: from IoT and smartphones to data centers and supercomputers. 

With profiles optimized for applications, real-time, and microcontrollers, Arm delivers scalability, energy efficiency, security, and broad ecosystem support unmatched by other processor architectures.

To overcome these limitations, Acorn Computers started developing its own RISC processor, leading to the creation of the ARM1 in 1985.

The ARM1 chip is built from functional blocks, each with a different purpose. Registers store data, the ALU (arithmetic-logic unit) performs simple arithmetic, instruction decoders determine how to handle each instruction, and so forth. Compared to most processors, the layout of the chip is simple, with each functional block clearly visible. (In comparison, the layout of chips such as the 6502 or Z-80 is highly hand-optimized to avoid any wasted space. In these chips, the functional blocks are squished together, making it harder to pick out the pieces.)

<TextWithImage
  body="Released in 1986, the ARM2 transitioned the architecture from the experimental ARM1 prototype into a commercial production success for the Acorn Archimedes. While maintaining the core RISC philosophy of simplicity, its major structural evolution was the addition of a dedicated hardware multiplier. Where the ARM1 relied on slow, multi-step software loops managed by control logic, the ARM2 allocated physical silicon space to accelerate multiplication and multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations directly. This generation retained the layout principles of the ARM1 but packed them densely to expand the transistor count from 25,000 to roughly 30,000."
  imageSrc={arm2.src}
  imageAlt="Image of an ARM2"
  imageCaption="ARM2"
/>

The ARM2 was first shipped running at a clock rate of 8 MHz, but even at this relatively sedate speed still delivered about 4 MIPS when attached to (slow) 120 ns commercial dynamic RAM. Since then, ARM2 has been commercially offered in 10 MHz and 12 MHz speeds, still yielding a performance of about half the clock rate in MIPS. This efficiency allowed the platform to deliver exceptional throughput for its era while maintaining the ultra-low power consumption and minimal thermal footprint that ultimately defined the architecture's lasting legacy.

<TextWithImage
  body="The third ARM revision, the ARM3, was a significant upgrade in both design and fabrication technology. It was also the first time that the Variants of the ARM CPU 167 macrocell approach had been used in the design of an ARM processor. The ARM3 exploits the small size of the ARM2 CPU core and adds to it a 4 kbyte on-chip cache which provides cached data in a single clock cycle. At the same time, the fabrication process was shrunk another couple of steps to allow a significant increase in clock rate."

The ARM3 comprises some 300 000 transistors, more than ten times as many as ARM2 and yet still small by contemporary microprocessor standards (the Intel 80486DX has roughly 1200 000). ARM3 is capable of operating at 25 MHz when fabricated in a 1.2 μm process and at 33 MHz when fabricated in a 0.8 μm process. At these speeds, it typically yields some 13-15 MIPS, yet still requires only relatively slow commercial DRAM.

The transition from ARM2 to ARM3 marked a doubling down on the Reduced Instruction Set Computing (RISC) philosophy. Unlike traditional processors that rely on complex, variable-length instructions requiring heavy hardware decoding, ARM utilizes fixed-length, simple instructions. This design allows the processor to execute operations in a single clock cycle with minimal transistor overhead. Because each instruction is uniform, the chip requires less power and generates significantly less heat than contemporary CISC-based architectures, establishing the efficiency footprint that remains ARM's core advantage.

## ARM Cortex Cores

The ARM Cortex family spans a full range of performance and power tradeoffs, with each sub-family built for a different job. Cortex-M handles low-power microcontrollers and embedded systems like wearables and IoT sensors, using the compact Thumb instruction set to fit 32-bit performance into tight memory and power budgets. Cortex-R prioritizes deterministic, fault-tolerant real-time response over raw speed, making it suited to safety-critical systems such as automotive ABS, industrial controllers, and aerospace. Cortex-A and Cortex-X both target full-OS devices like phones and laptops, balancing performance and battery life differently, and are often combined through big.LITTLE architecture so a single device can idle efficiently and still burst to high performance when needed.

<ImageGallery photos={photos} client:load />

<br />Together, these four families form a continuous ladder, from ultra-low-power microcontrollers, to guaranteed-response embedded control, to everyday mobile computing, to peak-performance flagship processing, all built on a shared instruction-set architecture. This range is what has made ARM cores foundational across such a wide span of modern computing, powering devices from simple fitness trackers to high-end smartphones.

##  ARM Neoverse

<TextWithImage
  body="As the most recent major expansion to the ARM family, the ARM Neoverse platform extends this architectural blueprint far beyond mobile devices, anchoring ARM's footprint in cloud computing, networking, and enterprise infrastructure."
  imageSrc={armneo.src}
  imageAlt="Image of an ARM Neoverse processor"
  imageCaption="An ARM Neoverse processor"
/>

Instead of powering personal electronics, Neoverse processors are meticulously optimized for servers, data centers, edge computing, and high-performance networking equipment. By focusing on high throughput, energy efficiency, and massive scalability, they allow cloud providers to seamlessly handle millions of simultaneous requests while drastically reducing total power consumption. Ultimately, as the global demand for cloud services and artificial intelligence continues to grow, Neoverse has solidified its role as a key component in modern server infrastructure and supercomputing environments.
