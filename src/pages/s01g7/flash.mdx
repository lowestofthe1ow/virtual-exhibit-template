<section id="intro">
  <h2>Introduction</h2>
  <p>
    Every storage medium before this one had a motor. Punch cards were fed through
    rollers, drums spun, platters rotated, discs whirled under a laser. Each new
    generation got faster, but every one of them still depended on something
    physical moving into position before a single bit could be read. In 1991,
    SanDisk shipped the first commercial <strong>solid-state drive</strong> &mdash; a
    20 MB unit with no motor at all. Data was trapped as electrical charge inside
    silicon, and for the first time, "seeking" a piece of data meant nothing: every
    cell was equally close, reachable in the same instant regardless of where it
    physically sat on the chip.
  </p>

  <figure class="figure-placeholder">
    <img src={earlySsd.src} alt="Photo of an early solid-state flash storage card" class="figure-img figure-img-flash" />
    <figcaption>Fig. Early solid-state flash storage, decades before SSDs replaced hard drives <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_drive" target="_blank" rel="noopener">(Wikipedia)</a></figcaption>
  </figure>

  <p>
    It took almost two decades for flash to become cheap enough to replace the hard
    drive in everyday computers, held back less by the technology itself than by
    the cost of manufacturing enough silicon to compete with a spinning platter's
    price per gigabyte. But once <strong>NAND flash</strong> caught up in price, it
    changed the shape of the memory hierarchy &mdash; closing the long-standing gap
    between fast, volatile RAM and slow, mechanical secondary storage, and setting
    up almost every other technology covered in this exhibit.
  </p>

  <PriceCrossoverRace client:visible />

  <p>
    What made that leap possible sits inside a single transistor, repeated across
    a chip billions of times over. The rest of this exhibit works outward from
    that one component &mdash; how a single cell traps and releases charge, how
    those cells are organized into a working chip, how that chip is protected from
    wearing out, and how the interface connecting it to the rest of the computer
    had to be reinvented just to keep up with what flash could actually do.
  </p>
</section>

<section id="nand">
  <h2>NAND Flash Memory Architecture</h2>
  <p>
    A NAND flash chip stores data in <strong>floating-gate transistors</strong> — a
    normal transistor with a second, electrically isolated gate buried inside it.
    Trapping electrons on that floating gate raises the cell's threshold voltage;
    removing them lowers it. Because the gate is insulated, the charge stays put
    with no power applied — this is what makes flash <strong>non-volatile</strong>.
  </p>

  <FloatingGateAnimation client:visible />

  <p>
    That single cell is the smallest unit of storage on the chip, but it never
    stands alone. Millions of them are wired together into a hierarchy that runs
    from the whole package all the way down to one transistor, and each level in
    that hierarchy exists to solve a different engineering problem — how much
    silicon fits in a package, how a controller addresses a specific location, and
    how much data can be written or erased in a single operation.
  </p>

  <NandHierarchyFlow client:visible />
  <p class="nand-caveat">
    Bar widths are on a log scale to fit five orders of magnitude on one chart &mdash;
    they show containment order (package &sup; die &sup; block &sup; page &sup; cell),
    not literal proportional size.
  </p>

  <p>
    Cells are organized into <strong>pages</strong> (the smallest unit that can be
    written) and pages are grouped into <strong>blocks</strong> (the smallest unit
    that can be erased). A cell can only hold one voltage state at a time, so more
    bits per cell means finer voltage steps packed into the same physical range —
    and the finer those steps get, the more precisely the controller has to read
    a cell's charge to tell one state from its neighbor:
  </p>
  <CellVoltageDiagram client:visible />
  <p>
    NAND has one quirk that shapes everything above it: it can write to an empty
    page, but it <strong>cannot overwrite</strong> a page directly &mdash; the whole block
    must be erased first. An SSD controller works around this by writing updates to
    a fresh page elsewhere and marking the old one invalid, then later copying the
    still-valid pages out of a block and erasing it wholesale. This background
    process is called <strong>garbage collection</strong>, and it's the reason a
    drive that's nearly full often feels slower than one with room to spare — the
    controller has fewer clean blocks to write into and has to do more shuffling
    to free new ones.
  </p>
</section>

<section id="lifespan">
  <h2>Wear Leveling and Flash Lifespan</h2>
  <p>
    Each erase cycle stresses the oxide layer around the floating gate a little
    more. Eventually a cell can no longer reliably hold a charge, so every NAND
    cell has a finite budget of <strong>program/erase (P/E) cycles</strong> &mdash;
    roughly 100,000 for SLC, down to just a few hundred for QLC, a gap of two to
    three orders of magnitude between the most and least durable cell types. That
    budget is why "how many bits per cell" isn't just a capacity and cost question
    &mdash; it's also a direct trade against how long the drive will last under
    sustained writes.
  </p>

  <p>
    If a drive always rewrote the same "hot" blocks &mdash; a swap file, a database log &mdash;
    those blocks would wear out in weeks while the rest of the chip sat untouched.
    <strong>Wear leveling</strong> algorithms prevent this by spreading writes
    evenly across the whole chip, trading a small amount of controller overhead
    for a dramatic increase in the drive's usable lifespan. There are two
    complementary strategies at work:
  </p>

  <WearLevelingAnimation client:visible />

  <p>
    Neither strategy works in isolation. Dynamic leveling alone would still leave
    long-idle data pinned to the same worn blocks forever, and static leveling
    alone would move data around unnecessarily on a drive that's already writing
    evenly. Together, they keep every block's wear count within a narrow band of
    every other block's, which is what lets a drive advertise a single endurance
    rating instead of "some blocks might die next week."
  </p>

  <p>
    Drives also reserve a slice of raw capacity that the user never sees, called
    <strong>over-provisioning</strong>, to give the controller room to juggle
    garbage collection and wear leveling without running out of free blocks. The
    <strong>TRIM</strong> command lets the operating system tell the drive which
    pages are no longer in use, so the controller can erase them ahead of time
    instead of scrambling during a write.
  </p>
</section>

<section id="interfaces">
  <h2>SATA vs. NVMe Interfaces</h2>
  <p>
    An SSD is only as fast as the road connecting it to the CPU. Early SSDs were
    built into the 2.5" form factor and spoke <strong>SATA</strong> &mdash; a protocol
    and connector designed in 2003 for spinning platters. SATA III tops out around
    6 Gbps (about 550 MB/s after overhead), and its command layer, <strong>AHCI</strong>,
    supports exactly one command queue, 32 commands deep &mdash; plenty for a mechanical
    drive that could barely keep that queue full, but a hard ceiling for flash, which
    can service far more requests at once than a spinning platter ever could.
  </p>

  <InterfacePathDiagram />

  <p>
    That single queue is the bottleneck the whole interface was built around, because
    AHCI was never designed with flash in mind &mdash; it inherited its assumptions
    from decades of hard drives, where one slow mechanical arm made a deep queue
    pointless anyway. Flash broke that assumption the moment it arrived, and for
    years SSDs were stuck running at a fraction of their real potential simply
    because the road connecting them to the CPU hadn't caught up with the drive
    itself.
  </p>

  <p>
    <strong>NVMe</strong> (Non-Volatile Memory Express) throws out that legacy path
    entirely. It talks straight to the CPU over <strong>PCIe lanes</strong> instead
    of a storage-specific bus, and its command set was designed from scratch around
    what flash can actually do: up to <strong>65,535 queues</strong>, each up to
    <strong>65,536 commands</strong> deep, so a modern multi-core CPU can issue
    requests to the drive in parallel instead of standing in one line. Every core
    can effectively own its own queue, which is exactly the kind of parallelism
    AHCI had no way to express.
  </p>

  <BusSpeedRace client:visible />

  <p>
    The gap isn't just bandwidth &mdash; it's overhead. Every AHCI command travels
    through a driver stack built for disks that took milliseconds to respond. NVMe
    strips that stack down to the operations flash actually needs, cutting CPU
    cycles spent per command and letting drives fully exploit PCIe's lanes as
    generations move from PCIe 3.0 to 4.0 and beyond.
  </p>
</section>

<section id="performance">
  <h2>Storage Performance: Latency and IOPS</h2>
  <p>
    Two numbers describe how "fast" a drive really feels in everyday use, and
    neither of them is the big MB/s figure on the box. Sequential throughput
    matters for copying one large file, but most real workloads &mdash; opening
    an app, loading a level, running a database query &mdash; are made of many
    small, scattered requests, and that's where these two numbers actually
    decide how responsive a drive feels.
  </p>
  <ul>
    <li>
      <strong>Latency</strong> &mdash; the delay before a single request starts
      returning data. An HDD pays for a physical seek and rotational delay, often
      several milliseconds, because a physical arm has to travel to the right
      spot on a spinning platter before it can read anything. Flash has no moving
      parts to wait for, so NVMe latency is typically measured in
      <strong>microseconds</strong> instead.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>IOPS</strong> (Input/Output Operations Per Second) &mdash; how many
      individual read or write operations a drive can complete per second,
      especially with small, random requests. This is the metric that matters for
      databases, virtual machines, and boot times, where the workload is thousands
      of tiny scattered requests rather than one long stream, and where a drive's
      advertised sequential speed says almost nothing about how it will actually
      perform.
    </li>
  </ul>
  <p>
    A 7,200 RPM HDD manages roughly 100&ndash;200 random IOPS, limited by how fast its
    arm can physically move from one track to the next. A SATA SSD reaches tens of
    thousands, already a leap of two to three orders of magnitude with no moving
    parts left to slow it down. An NVMe drive, freed from AHCI's single queue, can
    push past a million &mdash; not because flash reads any single cell faster
    than that, but because thousands of requests can be in flight across its
    parallel queues at once, arriving faster than any one queue could ever drain
    them.
  </p>

  <IopsGaugeCluster client:visible />
  <p class="nand-caveat">Log-scaled again — NVMe's real advantage over a spinning HDD is on the order of 10,000&times;, not the ~7&times; the bar lengths alone would suggest.</p>
</section>
