<section id="intro">
  <h2>Introduction</h2>
  <p>
    Magnetic storage was fast but fragile: disks warped, drives crashed, tapes degraded.
    In 1982, Sony and Philips answered with the <strong>Compact Disc</strong>: 650 MB on
    a coaster-sized disc, read by light alone, never touching the surface. It outlasted
    every magnetic medium of its era.
  </p>
  <p>
    Over 30 years, optical storage evolved through three generations: CD (1982),
    DVD (1995), and Blu-ray (2006), each squeezing more data onto the same disc by
    using shorter and shorter wavelengths of laser light.
  </p>

  <p class="gen-caption">Three generations, one disc size &mdash; each read by a shorter wavelength than the last.</p>
  <p>
    Optical discs weren't the first medium to encode information as light-readable
    marks. The <strong>LaserDisc</strong>, launched in 1978, pioneered the idea of a
    reflective analog video disc read by laser. But LaserDiscs were bulky (the size of
    a vinyl record) and analog, storing video as a continuously varying signal rather
    than discrete bits. The CD's breakthrough was combining the laser-reading concept
    with fully <strong>digital</strong> encoding, governed by the Red Book standard
    Sony and Philips published in 1980, the first time an optical format's physical
    and logical specifications were locked down before mass production, letting every
    manufacturer's discs play in every manufacturer's player.
  </p>
</section>

  <p>
    Data is stamped into the polycarbonate as a spiral of microscopic bumps called
    <strong>pits</strong>, separated by flat areas called <strong>lands</strong>.
    A <strong>laser diode</strong> shines through the base onto the track: lands reflect
    the beam back cleanly (high signal), while pit edges scatter it (low signal).
    Those highs and lows become binary data.
  </p>

  <h3>Why Wavelength Matters</h3>
  <p>
    A laser can only resolve features about half its wavelength in size. Shorter = smaller
    pits = more data. CD uses <strong>780 nm</strong> (infrared), DVD uses <strong>650 nm</strong>
    (red), and Blu-ray uses <strong>405 nm</strong> (violet), which is why capacity jumps
    so dramatically across formats.
  </p>

<section id="pits-lands">
  <h2>Pits and Lands as Binary Data</h2>
  <p>
    Common misconception: pits ≠ 1s and lands ≠ 0s. On real CDs, it's the
    <strong>transitions</strong> between pit and land that represent a
    <code style="color:var(--accent-green)">1</code>. Staying on either surface
    (no transition) is a <code style="color:var(--accent-green)">0</code>.
    This is called <strong>NRZI</strong> encoding.
  </p>

    which converts each byte into a 14-bit pattern, guaranteeing no run of identical signal
    is too long or too short for the optics to track.
  </p>

<section id="error-correction">
  <h2>Error Correction: Surviving Scratches and Dust</h2>
  <p>
    A CD spins at up to 500 RPM under a laser reading marks measured in hundredths of a
    micron, so a single fingerprint smudge, dust particle, or hairline scratch can wipe out
    thousands of consecutive bits. Optical media survives this because every byte written
    to the disc is protected by <strong>CIRC</strong> (Cross-Interleaved Reed-Solomon Code),
    developed for the Red Book standard alongside EFM.
  </p>
  <p>
    CIRC works in two layers. First, a <strong>Reed-Solomon</strong> code adds parity symbols
    that let the player reconstruct missing or corrupted bytes through math alone, without
    ever needing to re-read the disc. Second, and just as important, the encoder
    <strong>interleaves</strong> the data, spreading each chunk of a file across
    widely separated physical locations on the spiral track before writing it. A scratch
    that destroys one contiguous stretch of the disc surface therefore only damages a few
    scattered bytes from many different chunks, each easily recoverable by Reed-Solomon,
    rather than annihilating one chunk completely.
  </p>
  <p>
    This is why a CD with a visible scratch across its surface will often still play
    perfectly, while a single deep scratch on a hard drive platter can be catastrophic.
    The interleaving trades a small amount of storage overhead for a large amount of
    physical resilience.
  </p>

  <p>
    The track pitch shrinks 5&times; from CD to Blu-ray, giving Blu-ray ~35&times; more
    capacity on the exact same diameter disc. The tradeoff: Blu-ray's thinner protective
    layer (0.1 mm vs. DVD's 0.6 mm) makes it more scratch-prone.
  </p>
  <p>
    Capacity could also be doubled without shrinking pits further, by stacking a second
    semi-transparent recording layer beneath the first. <strong>Dual-layer DVDs (DVD-9)</strong>
    hold 8.5 GB by refocusing the laser deeper into the disc for the second layer, and
    Blu-ray pushed the same trick to <strong>BD-XL</strong> discs with up to four layers
    and 128 GB, though each additional layer demands a more precise, more expensive laser
    assembly to focus through the layers above it without cross-talk.
  </p>
</section>

<section id="recordable">
  <h2>Recordable & Rewritable Discs</h2>
  <p>
    Factory-pressed discs like retail CDs have their pits physically stamped into the
    polycarbonate at manufacture, so there is no "writing" involved at all. Consumer
    recordable discs had to fake the same optical behavior using materials a laser
    could alter at home, which split the technology into two families.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>CD-R and DVD±R</strong> ("recordable") discs replace the reflective aluminum
    layer's neighbor with a layer of light-sensitive organic <strong>dye</strong>. A
    high-power write laser heats spots along the dye layer, permanently darkening them
    so they scatter light instead of reflecting it, mimicking a pit optically, even
    though the disc surface itself never actually deforms. Because the dye can't
    revert once burned, these discs are <strong>write-once</strong>: ideal for archival
    backups precisely because the data can't later be altered or accidentally erased.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>CD-RW and DVD±RW</strong> ("rewritable") discs use a <strong>phase-change
    alloy</strong> instead of dye, typically a silver-indium-antimony-tellurium
    compound. The write laser can heat this alloy to two different temperatures: a
    high heat that melts it into a disordered, light-scattering <em>amorphous</em>
    state (representing a pit), or a lower, sustained heat that lets it cool slowly
    back into an ordered, reflective <em>crystalline</em> state (erasing that spot back
    to a land). This reversible melt-and-anneal cycle is what lets rewritable discs be
    erased and rewritten roughly 1,000 times, at the cost of a dimmer reflectivity
    contrast than a stamped disc, which is why rewritable media generally demands a
    more sensitive, more expensive laser pickup to read reliably.
  </p>

<section id="decline">
  <h2>The Decline of Optical Media</h2>
  <p>
    Blu-ray, released in 2006, turned out to be the last mainstream optical format.
    Its arrival coincided almost exactly with two shifts that made physical discs feel
    increasingly unnecessary: consumer broadband made streaming video and downloadable
    software practical, and flash memory prices fell fast enough that USB drives and SD
    cards became cheaper, faster, rewritable, and far more durable per gigabyte than any
    disc could be.
  </p>
  <p>
    By the early 2010s, laptop manufacturers began shipping models without an optical
    drive at all, and software distribution moved to digital storefronts. Optical media
    never fully disappeared. It remains dominant in film physical-media collecting,
    professional archival workflows (thanks to CIRC's resilience and long dye/inorganic
    shelf life), and regions with limited broadband, but its three-decade run as the
    default medium for distributing music, movies, games, and software effectively ended
    within a decade of Blu-ray's launch.
  </p>
